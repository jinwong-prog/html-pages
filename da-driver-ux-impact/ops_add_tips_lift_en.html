Additional finding: even after controlling for the difficulty threshold, tipped orders are still harder to dispatch

  • Additional finding: even when comparing only orders that already met the same threshold (wait ≥120s, backlog ≥40 orders), tipped orders are still harder to dispatch than no-tip orders.
  • What the data shows: tipped orders have a median wait time of 560 seconds, versus 257 seconds for no-tip orders; median backlog is also higher (155 orders vs. 131 orders).
  • What this means: the dual-gate condition can only tell whether the dispatch threshold was met, not how difficult an order is beyond that threshold. Tipped orders are mostly the harder batch among those that qualify.
  • Therefore: the lower completion rate for tipped orders more likely reflects that these orders were inherently harder to complete, rather than tipping itself lowering the completion rate.
  • Recommendation: to fairly evaluate the effect of tipping, use a finer difficulty breakdown (e.g. actual wait time, actual backlog), or run an A/B test to verify tipping's true impact.

Summary and recommendations

  • Overall result: across all fare tiers, tipped orders have a lower completion rate than no-tip orders, with gaps of roughly 28–49 percentage points.
  • Effect of tip amount: no trend of "higher tip, higher completion rate" — raising the tip amount does not produce a consistent improvement.
  • Consistency: across 17 day-by-fare-tier comparison cells, not a single one shows tipping outperforming no-tip.
  • Rule check: no evidence of high-fare orders being over-subsidized; however, 305 orders received a tip amount below what the sheet's rule calls for — the cause should be confirmed.
  • Financial impact: tipped, completed orders still net a positive $8.60 per order on average, though lower than the $18.44 for no-tip orders; no case was found where the tip exceeded the order's revenue.
  • Residual difficulty confounding: even within the "same difficulty" subset, tipped orders still have a noticeably higher median wait time and backlog than no-tip orders (see the additional finding above) — tips are likely assigned to the hardest orders within that difficulty bucket, so part of the observed completion-rate gap may reflect selection bias rather than the effect of tipping itself.
  • Recommendation: the current results only reflect correlation and cannot prove that tipping causes the completion-rate difference; a formal A/B test or randomized controlled trial (RCT) is recommended to verify the effect.
  • Data limitation: the data still contains tipped orders with a fare ≥ $200, which is inconsistent with the sheet's rule; separately, BigQuery cannot verify "has not previously received a one-time tip," so this was not included in the analysis.
