Long-wait orders (over 400 sec) show no improvement either

Orders waiting over 400 sec are the hardest to dispatch and most in need of rescue. But in all three fare bands the subsidized group still has a lower completion rate (<$85 lower by 17.2 percentage points, $150–199 lower by 10.9, $85–149 lower by 2.3). Even for the hardest-to-dispatch orders, subsidies did not help.

③ Why is this happening? — Subsidies are given to harder orders in the first place

It is not that subsidies are necessarily useless, but that subsidized orders are inherently harder to dispatch. Here we use Backlog (order backlog: the number of orders competing for drivers at the moment of dispatch; higher means harder to dispatch) to see this: even at the same wait time, the subsidized group has a higher Backlog in every group. That is, the actual difficulty of the two groups is not the same, and comparing completion rates directly is unfair to the subsidized group.

Wastage100% (est.)Wastage = the share of paid subsidies that "bought no provable incremental completion" = (paid − effective subsidies)/paid; effective subsidies = incremental completions × subsidy per order. Since incremental completions ≤ 0 (none observed), effective subsidies = $0, so = $7,120 ÷ $7,120 = 100%. Capped at 100% (you cannot spend negative money); the point estimate being negative suggests harder orders were selected — a selection bias, see ③
What is "extra orders rescued (incremental completions)"? Assuming no subsidy had been given, how many of these orders would have been cancelled but were completed only because a subsidy was added — i.e. the orders the subsidy genuinely rescued.
Incremental completions is not a number you can directly count in real life (no one can tell which specific order "would have been cancelled without the subsidy") — it is estimated using the adjusted completion-rate gap: 
Adjusted completion-rate gap = subsidized-group completion rate − no-subsidy-group completion rate,
                               after controlling for fare / wait time / backlog
                             = 55.3% − 62.6%
                             = −1.9pp (i.e. −1.9%)

Incremental completions = subsidized orders × adjusted completion-rate gap
                        = 1,255 × (−1.9%)
                        ≈ −24 (negative, i.e. no evidence of extra completions)
The result is ≤ 0, i.e. no incremental completions observed.
Because no incremental completions were observed (estimate −58 to −117, with a 95% confidence interval crossing 0), "cost per order rescued" cannot be computed, and "wastage" can only be conservatively estimated at 100% (i.e. at this stage no provable effective subsidy spend can be seen). For a reliable ROI and Wastage, we first need pre-treatment matching or an A/B test to confirm that the subsidized group's completion rate is genuinely higher than the no-subsidy group's (see the recommended approach in ⑤).

⑤ Next steps: how can we actually know whether subsidies work?

What can be said: no lift in completion rate from Subsidy was observed; and the subsidized batch of orders is inherently harder to dispatch (higher backlog).
But we cannot conclude "subsidies are entirely useless" — region, vehicle type, order-placement time, driver supply and so on are not yet fully aligned and may still affect the result.
To answer "is it worth increasing Budget", we recommend:
  • Match and compare using conditions already known before dispatch (backlog, fare, vehicle type, region, order-placement time), and do not use wait time — wait time only appears after dispatch, so using it for matching distorts the result.
  • Or run an A/B experiment (random assignment) directly — the cleanest way to see whether Subsidy truly lifts the completion rate.

Method: orders with similar fare, wait time and order backlog are compared side by side, and the subsidy cost per completed order and the wastage rate (the share of subsidies that did not lead to an additional completion) are estimated.
